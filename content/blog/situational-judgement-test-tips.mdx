The situational judgement test is usually the first gate in a graduate or early-career application: a series of workplace scenarios, each with four or five possible responses, which you rank, rate or pick from. No numbers, no verbal comprehension, no preparation materials that feel like they help. Just judgement, measured at scale.

Because SJTs feel unpreparable, most candidates take them cold. They are not unpreparable. They measure something consistent, the scoring works in a knowable way, and the difference between a passing and failing sitting is usually a handful of questions.

## What an SJT actually measures

Each scenario encodes a small conflict between values an employer cares about: pace versus accuracy, honesty versus loyalty, initiative versus escalation, the customer versus the process. The response options are calibrated by the employer (usually by asking experienced staff to rank them), and your score reflects how closely your judgement matches that calibration.

This is the key insight: an SJT is not asking what would you do. It is asking do you understand how this organisation expects its people to behave. Those are related but not identical, and the gap between them is where marks are lost.

## Why the obvious answer sometimes is not the credited one

Take a classic scenario: you notice a colleague has made an error in something already sent to a client. Options range from tell them privately, through tell your manager immediately, to say nothing since it is not your responsibility.

Candidates who optimise for loyalty pick the quiet word. Candidates who optimise for hierarchy escalate at once. What is usually credited is the sequence that fixes the problem while respecting the person: flag it to the colleague so they can correct it, and escalate only if the risk is live and the correction does not happen. The test rewards judgement about ordering, not just direction.

Common credited patterns across employers:

- **Address problems directly with the person involved before escalating**, unless safety, legality or a customer is at immediate risk.
- **Take ownership of the piece you can act on now** rather than only reporting upwards.
- **Never ignore, hide or delay** a problem: the say nothing option is almost always the anchored bottom of the ranking.
- **Protect the customer or service user first** when their interests conflict with internal convenience.

## How scoring works

Most SJTs use partial credit against the calibrated ranking, so near-miss judgements still score. This has two practical consequences. First, do not agonise over adjacent options; the cost of swapping second and third is small. Spend your care on identifying the best and worst options, where the scoring spread is largest. Second, consistency beats brilliance: candidates fail SJTs by being erratic, not by lacking genius.

Some tests (notably in the [Civil Service](/blog/civil-service-online-tests) and NHS) blend SJT items with strengths or values items. The approach holds: the organisation has published what it values, and the credited answers track it.

## How to prepare

**Read what the employer says about itself.** Values pages and competency frameworks are the calibration key in plain sight. An SJT for a safety-critical engineering firm and one for a fast-moving retailer credit different answers to the same scenario, and both tell you in advance.

**Do one full practice test for format familiarity.** Question banks have diminishing returns because scenarios do not repeat, but one timed sitting removes the surprise of ranking mechanics and pacing.

**Slow down on the worst option.** Identifying the clearly wrong behaviour is the most reliable scoring move on ranked items, and it is usually easier than picking the best.

**Answer as your best professional self, consistently.** Not the self you fear you would be at 5pm on a bad Friday, and not a fantasy hero who confronts a director on day one. The same sensible, direct, ownership-taking professional, question after question.

## Where the SJT fits in the pipeline

Passing the SJT usually feeds you into [online tests](/blog/psychometric-test-tips), a [video interview](/blog/how-to-ace-a-video-interview) and then an [assessment centre](/blog/what-to-expect-at-an-assessment-centre). The judgement the SJT samples in multiple choice form is the same judgement interviews sample in story form, so preparation compounds: a candidate who can explain a good decision aloud rarely misreads the multiple choice version of it.

That spoken half is where [practice interviews](/practice) earn their keep: rehearsing decisions aloud, with scoring on the reasoning, trains exactly the muscle the SJT samples silently.
