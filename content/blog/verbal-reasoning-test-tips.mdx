Verbal reasoning tests look like the friendly half of the online testing stage: no maths, just short passages and statements to judge. Then strong readers, people with essay-based degrees, sit one and score worse than they did on the numbers. It happens constantly, and it happens for a specific reason.

Verbal reasoning is not a reading test. It is a discipline test: can you judge a statement using only the passage in front of you, with everything you otherwise know switched off. The better read you are, the more outside knowledge is available to contaminate your answers.

## The format

You get a short passage (150 to 250 words, usually on a deliberately dry topic) and three to five statements. For each statement you answer:

- **True**: the passage says this, explicitly or by direct logical consequence.
- **False**: the passage contradicts this.
- **Cannot say**: the passage does not contain enough information to decide either way.

Sixty to ninety seconds per statement. The passages rotate: business, science, policy, none requiring expertise, all chosen so that plausible outside knowledge exists to tempt you.

## Cannot say, done properly

Nearly all lost marks concentrate on the third option, in both directions.

**Choosing true because it is true in the world.** The statement is plausible, you know it to be broadly correct, the passage merely fails to say it. The answer is cannot say. The test is measuring exactly whether you can feel that difference.

**Choosing cannot say out of timidity.** If the passage says the scheme reduced costs in every region surveyed, then the statement the scheme reduced costs in the northern region is true, not cannot say, provided the north was surveyed. Direct logical consequence counts as stated. Cannot say is not there is some conceivable doubt; it is the passage genuinely does not address this.

A working rule: True needs the passage to say it. False needs the passage to fight it. Cannot say is silence. Argue only from what is on the page, as if the passage were the only document in the world.

## A technique that holds up under the clock

1. **Read the statement first.** It tells you what to hunt for and saves a full read of a passage you will only need a third of.
2. **Find the relevant sentences** and reread those precisely: quantifiers (all, most, some), tense, and hedges (may, could, aims to) decide most answers.
3. **Judge against the words, not the vibe.** A passage that aims to halve emissions has not halved emissions. A policy that applies to most branches says nothing certain about yours.
4. **Answer and move.** Lingering after a decision is the most common pacing leak. Your reviewed first judgement is right far more often than your anxious second one.

Watch specifically for scope-shift traps, where the passage discusses one group (UK retailers) and the statement quietly widens it (retailers). The widened claim is cannot say, and the test writers know how invisible that shift is at speed.

## Preparing

The skill builds faster than numerical fluency: most candidates see real accuracy gains inside a week of daily 15-minute timed sets. Review errors by asking, each time, which word in the passage decided this. If you cannot point to a word, you argued from outside knowledge, and that habit is the thing to train out.

As with numerical tests, find out the publisher if you can and practise their style, and treat an unsupervised sitting honestly: scores are commonly re-verified at the [assessment centre](/blog/what-to-expect-at-an-assessment-centre).

The habit verbal reasoning trains (claims judged strictly against evidence) is not test trivia. It is the same discipline [case study exercises](/blog/assessment-centre-case-study-tips) score as using the exhibits, and interviews score as answering the question that was asked. Precision with what was actually said is a career skill; the test just measures it early. For the spoken version of that precision, [practice interviews with scored feedback](/practice) will tell you when your answers drift from the question, which is the same fault in a different costume.
