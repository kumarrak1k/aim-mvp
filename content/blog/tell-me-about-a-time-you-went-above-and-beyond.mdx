Most people misjudge the scale of this question in one of two directions. Either they reach for something enormous and heroic that did not really happen, and it shows, or they pick something so small that "above and beyond" is a stretch: they stayed ten minutes late once. Neither works.

What the interviewer actually means by "above and beyond" is narrower and more interesting than the phrase suggests. They want a moment where you did something you were not asked to do and did not have to do, because you noticed it needed doing and cared about the outcome more than the boundary of your role. The size is almost irrelevant. The noticing is the whole thing.

## The two ingredients that matter

Strip the phrase down and a strong answer has exactly two components:

- **It was genuinely optional.** Nobody would have blamed you for not doing it. If it was expected, or someone told you to, it is not above and beyond, it is just your job.
- **It came from you spotting something.** The best versions start with an observation nobody else acted on. That is the part that shows initiative and care, rather than someone performing extra effort to look good.

If you can point to the moment you noticed the gap, and confirm that closing it was optional, you have the answer. Everything else is delivery.

## A worked example

> "I was working part-time in a bookshop while at university, mostly on the till. We had a lot of older regulars, and I noticed one of them, a man who came in every week, had stopped being able to manage the stairs to the second floor where we kept the large-print and audiobooks he liked. He had started just buying whatever was downstairs instead, and you could tell he was a bit embarrassed about it.
>
> Nobody asked me to do anything about it. But I started keeping a note of what he tended to like, and when he came in I would have already brought a few options down for him to look at near the till. It took me maybe five minutes a week.
>
> He mentioned it to the manager, unprompted, and it turned into a small thing we did for a couple of other regulars too, a little shelf of held-back titles for people who could not get upstairs easily. It was not some big initiative, it just made those customers' visits work, and they kept coming.
>
> Honestly I did it because the alternative, watching him quietly give up on the books he actually wanted, felt worse than the five minutes. But it taught me that the small things you notice about people are often the ones that matter most to them."

## Why it works

The scale is deliberately modest, five minutes a week, and that is a strength, not a weakness. It is believable, it is clearly optional, and it starts with a genuine observation that nobody else made. The candidate is not claiming to have saved the company. They noticed a person struggling and quietly solved it, and it spread because it was good.

The last line is doing careful work. "I did it because the alternative felt worse" reframes the whole thing away from "look how dedicated I am" and towards "I pay attention to people", which is a more attractive quality and a harder one to fake. Nobody remembers the candidate who worked the hardest. They remember the one who noticed the man on the stairs.

## The traps

**The invented epic.** Grand stories where you single-handedly rescued a launch tend to collapse under a follow-up question, because the details were never real. Interviewers ask "what exactly did you do" and the seams show. Pick something true and small over something impressive and vague.

**No proportion.** On the flip side, if your example is doing your actual job slightly well, it will not register as above and beyond. Staying late to finish your own task is diligence. Spotting someone else's problem and quietly fixing it is the thing.

**Doing it for the credit.** If the answer is clearly about being seen to go the extra mile, it reads as performance. The strongest versions are things you did that nobody would necessarily have noticed. That is what makes them credible.

**Skipping the trigger.** Do not start at "so I did this extra thing". Start at the moment you noticed the gap. The observation is the most impressive part of the story and the part people cut.

## Where to look for yours

Any job, any volunteering, any group where you noticed something falling through a crack and quietly caught it: a customer who needed more than the transaction, a new starter nobody was helping, a process that was annoying everyone and you fixed. The [STAR framework](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) handles all of them. Choose the one where the "why" is about care rather than credit.

The tell that separates a real above-and-beyond story from a stretched one is whether the "beyond" was optional. Before your interview, say your answer aloud and ask yourself honestly: would anyone have minded if I had not done it. If the answer is no, you have picked well. You can [pressure-test it](/practice) against that exact question and hear whether it holds up.
