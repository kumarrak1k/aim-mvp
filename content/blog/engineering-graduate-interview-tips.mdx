Engineering graduate interviews (at consultancies, contractors, manufacturers, utilities, rail and aerospace firms) blend a technical conversation with a standard competency interview, then frequently add commercial and safety dimensions that catch technically excellent candidates off guard. This guide covers the mix, and where the marks concentrate. (Software engineering runs a different process, covered [separately](/blog/software-engineer-interview-tips).)

## The shape of the process

A typical pipeline: online application and [tests](/blog/psychometric-test-tips), a [video or phone interview](/blog/how-to-ace-a-video-interview), then an [assessment centre](/blog/what-to-expect-at-an-assessment-centre) or technical interview day. Many engineering employers include a group exercise built around a design or logistics problem and a short presentation, sometimes on your degree project.

The technical interview is rarely an exam. Interviewers (usually practising engineers) want a conversation about things you have genuinely done: your final-year project, a placement, a design-build-test module. Depth on your own work beats breadth across the syllabus.

## Talking about projects properly

The default graduate mistake is describing the artefact (we designed a footbridge) rather than the engineering. What interviewers listen for:

- **Decisions and trade-offs.** Why that material, that configuration, that method? What did you optimise for, and what did you sacrifice? An answer with a trade-off in it sounds like an engineer.
- **What went wrong.** Every real project hits problems. The candidate who explains a failure, the diagnosis and the fix is demonstrating the actual job. Polished projects with no setbacks read as either shallow involvement or shallow honesty.
- **Numbers where they belong.** Loads, tolerances, efficiency gains, cost. Precision signals ownership.
- **Your specific contribution** in group projects. I led the analysis of X is checkable and credible; we did everything together is neither.

Prepare your two best projects to that pattern, aloud, and expect follow-up questions probing whichever detail you mention: mention a safety factor and you will be asked how it was chosen.

## The dimensions graduates underprepare

**Safety.** For contractors, rail, energy and aerospace, safety is a scored value, not small talk. Have an example of noticing and acting on a risk (a lab, a site placement, even a part-time job), and never treat a safety question as a formality to hurry past.

**Commercial awareness.** Engineering firms make money under contracts, and graduate interviews increasingly test whether you know it: why projects overrun, what a client relationship involves, roughly how your discipline's industry is structured. Our [commercial awareness guide](/blog/commercial-awareness-interview-guide) covers the method; apply it to your sector's trade press for a fortnight before the interview.

**Chartership.** Know the route (EngTech, IEng, CEng through the relevant institution), and be able to say how the employer's graduate scheme supports it. It signals you are planning a career, not hunting a job.

## The behavioural half

The competency interview is standard and standardly weighted: teamwork, pressure, problem-solving, communication, told in [STAR shape](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) with results attached. Engineering-flavoured versions of the classics recur: a disagreement about a technical approach ([conflict guide](/blog/tell-me-about-a-time-you-disagreed-with-your-manager)), a deadline threatened by a technical setback, explaining something complex to a non-technical audience. That last one is worth explicit practice: pick a concept from your degree and rehearse a two-minute explanation your grandmother could follow, because some version of it comes up constantly.

## Practising the mix

The technical conversation and the competency interview reward the same preparation method: spoken rehearsal with feedback. Run your project walkthroughs and competency stories in [practice interviews](/practice) (the technical interview type fits engineering roles well), and if your process includes an assessment centre, a [full mock run](/assessment-centre) covers the case study, interview and presentation stages in one sitting.

Engineering hiring is fundamentally sane: it wants evidence you can do engineering, work with people, and not hurt anyone. Prepare your own work deeply, the classics fluently, and the safety and commercial layers deliberately, and you are ahead of most of the queue.
