Of all the assessment centre exercises, role play produces the most dread per minute of exercise. You are put in a room (or a video call) with a trained actor or assessor playing an unhappy customer, a struggling team member or a resistant stakeholder, and asked to handle the conversation live.

The dread is understandable and mostly misplaced. Role play is not an acting test, and the candidates who do best are rarely the most theatrical. It is a behaviour test with a short list of behaviours, and it rewards preparation as reliably as any other exercise.

## Why employers use it

Interviews collect claims about behaviour: tell me about a time you handled a difficult customer. Role play collects the behaviour itself. For roles with real interpersonal load (management, sales, customer-facing work, HR, healthcare, the public sector) it is the highest-signal exercise in the schedule, which is why it keeps its place despite being expensive to run.

## The scenarios that come up

The surface details vary; the underlying shapes repeat:

- **The unhappy customer or service user**: something has gone wrong, they are frustrated, and policy will not let you give them everything they want.
- **The underperforming or overwhelmed colleague**: you manage or work alongside someone whose work has slipped, and the cause is not laziness.
- **The resistant stakeholder**: you need cooperation from someone with different priorities who has already said no once.
- **The delivery of bad news**: a decision has been made and your job is to communicate it without wrecking the relationship.

Notice that none of these can be won by force. Every scenario is built so that steamrolling fails, because the thing being measured is whether you can hold a relationship and a boundary at the same time.

## What assessors watch for

- **Listening before solving.** The strongest observable behaviour is asking questions and reflecting back what you heard before proposing anything. The actor is briefed to soften when genuinely listened to.
- **Empathy that is specific.** I can see this has cost you two weekends lands. I understand your frustration, said to the window, does not.
- **A boundary held cleanly.** Where the brief includes something you cannot offer, assessors watch whether you say so directly, without hiding behind policy language or over-apologising.
- **Progress.** The conversation should end somewhere: a concrete next step, an agreed action, a date. Sympathetic drift scores worse than imperfect progress.
- **Composure under provocation.** The actor may push. The push is the test. Slower speech and a lower register under pressure read as control.

## How to prepare without a script

You cannot script the conversation, but you can train the opening and the shape:

1. **Open with the other person, not the issue.** Thank them for meeting, then ask them to walk you through it from their side. This buys information and goodwill in the same sentence.
2. **Listen for the real complaint.** In most briefs the presenting complaint (the refund, the deadline) sits on top of a deeper one (feeling ignored, unfair treatment). Naming the deeper one changes the temperature.
3. **Trade, do not surrender.** Where you cannot give what is asked, offer what you can, and be honest about the gap.
4. **Close with an action.** Summarise what you both agreed, name who does what, put a time on it.

Read the brief for constraints before the meeting starts: what you can offer, what you cannot, and what the organisation needs from the relationship afterwards. Candidates who skim the brief give away things the brief forbids, and assessors notice.

## Practising the delivery

The skill that fails under pressure is rarely the plan; it is the delivery: pace, tone, and recovering when interrupted. That only improves by speaking, not reading.

Practise aloud with a friend playing awkward, or use our [voice and camera practice sessions](/practice), which score delivery, pace and composure on realistic workplace scenarios and give you feedback no friend will volunteer.
