The Fast Stream is the Civil Service's graduate development programme, and it is among the most competitive schemes in the country, with tens of thousands of applications for a comparatively small number of places. The process is long, largely online in its early stages, and unusually transparent: unlike most graduate schemes, the Civil Service publishes the framework it assesses against, which is a real advantage if you use it.

The single most common reason capable candidates do not progress is not ability. It is treating the early online stages as a formality to be rushed through before the "real" assessment, when in fact most of the field is cut there.

## The shape of the process

Exact stages and their order vary by year and by scheme, so always work from the current Fast Stream site and your own invitation emails rather than from a guide. That said, the process has been broadly consistent in recent cycles:

1. **Online application.** Eligibility, scheme preferences and basic details.
2. **Online tests.** Typically a work based scenarios exercise, a behaviours or motivation questionnaire, and an analytical or numerical reasoning element.
3. **Video interview.** Recorded, asynchronous, usually a mix of Behaviour and Strength questions.
4. **Fast Stream Assessment Centre (FSAC).** The final stage, historically a full day, in recent years often run virtually, combining several exercises.
5. **Scheme specific stages.** Some schemes, notably the analytical and digital ones, add technical assessments.

Applications typically open in the **autumn** and close within a few weeks, and the process then runs across several months. If you intend to apply this cycle, the practical implication is that preparation needs to happen before applications open, not after.

## Stage two: the online tests

These are sifting tools and they cut hard.

**Work based scenarios.** You are given a workplace situation and asked to rate or rank possible responses. This is a situational judgement test, and it is assessing whether your instincts match how the Civil Service expects people to operate: consulting appropriately, escalating at the right point, considering the wider impact, being straight with people. There is no way to fake this convincingly, but there is a great deal to be gained from understanding the value set being tested. The [situational judgement approach](/blog/how-to-answer-situational-interview-questions) transfers directly, and general [psychometric preparation](/blog/psychometric-test-tips) helps with timing and nerves.

**Behaviours and motivation questionnaires.** Consistency matters more than optimisation. These often contain checks for candidates who answer inconsistently across similar items, so answering as the person you think they want will tend to show up.

**Analytical elements.** Standard reasoning under time pressure. The main gains here come from familiarity rather than cleverness: doing timed practice so the format is not new, and learning to move on from a question you are stuck on rather than protecting your pride on it.

A practical point that costs people places every year: do these in a quiet block of time, on a proper screen, with a calculator and paper to hand where permitted. A meaningful number of candidates fail the tests because they attempted them on a phone between other commitments.

## Stage three: the video interview

Recorded interviews are uncomfortable because there is no interviewer to react to, and candidates either freeze or ramble. Two things help disproportionately.

First, treat the lack of an audience as a structural problem rather than an emotional one. Without a listener nodding along, you lose your natural cue to stop talking, which is why recorded answers tend to run long. Decide your structure before you start speaking and stop when you reach the end of it.

Second, prepare for the setup, not just the content. Camera at eye level, light in front of you rather than behind, a plain background, and a test recording watched back once. The [video interview guide](/blog/how-to-ace-a-video-interview) and the notes on [camera presence](/blog/camera-presence-for-interviews) cover this properly, and the [asynchronous interview guide](/blog/async-video-interview-tips) is specifically about the recorded format.

Expect Behaviour questions and Strength questions in the same sitting. They need different handling: Behaviours want a structured evidenced example, Strengths want a short, honest, present tense answer. Switching between the two modes is the part that catches people out.

## Stage four: the assessment centre

The FSAC is designed to observe you working rather than describing your work. It has historically combined several exercises, which in recent cycles have included a leadership or group exercise, a policy or briefing exercise involving analysis of a document pack, and a further interview element.

Two things are worth understanding about how it is marked.

**Assessors mark against the framework, not against each other's candidates.** You are not competing to dominate the room. In a group exercise, the behaviours that score are building on others' contributions, bringing in someone who has not spoken, managing time, and moving the group towards a decision. Talking most is not the same as scoring most, and candidates who treat it as a competition frequently mark themselves down. The [group exercise guide](/blog/group-exercise-interview-tips) covers this in detail.

**The written exercises test judgement under constraint, not writing flair.** In a policy or briefing exercise you will typically have more material than time. The skill being observed is deciding what matters, stating a clear recommendation, and being explicit about the trade offs and risks. A tidy, decisive one page note beats an elegant three page survey of the evidence that reaches no conclusion. The [in-tray exercise guide](/blog/in-tray-exercise-tips) and [case study guide](/blog/how-to-ace-a-case-study-interview) are the closest analogues.

## Where candidates most often fall short

**Rushing the early stages.** The tests eliminate the largest share of the field. They deserve preparation time proportionate to that.

**Preparing Behaviours but not Strengths.** Very common, and it shows immediately. See the [Strengths guide](/blog/civil-service-strengths-questions).

**Ignoring the published framework.** The Civil Service tells you what it is assessing. Reading the Success Profiles descriptors for the relevant level and writing to those words is free marks that most applicants leave on the table. Start with the [Behaviours breakdown](/blog/civil-service-behaviours-examples) and the [scoring guide](/blog/how-civil-service-applications-are-scored).

**Choosing schemes carelessly.** Scheme preferences matter, and some are far more competitive than others. Read what each actually involves rather than picking on title alone, and be honest about which suits how you like to work.

**Generic motivation.** "I want to make a difference" is what everyone writes. A specific reason grounded in a policy area, a piece of departmental work you have followed, or something concrete about how government operates is far stronger, and it is also more likely to be true.

## A realistic preparation plan

If applications open in the autumn, the useful sequencing is: spend August and early September on the framework and on building four or five strong Behaviour examples at the right level, then do timed test practice in the two or three weeks before you apply so the formats are familiar, then rehearse speaking your examples aloud before the video stage.

That last part is the one candidates skip and then regret, because written evidence and spoken evidence are different skills. An example that reads well on the form frequently comes out stiff, over long, or oddly defensive the first time it is spoken under pressure.

You can [practise Behaviour and Strength questions on camera and get feedback](/practice) on structure, length and delivery, which is the closest thing to the video interview stage short of the real one.
