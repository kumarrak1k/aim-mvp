The panic is always the same. You are reading through likely competency questions, "describe a time you handled a conflict", "give an example of when you led something", "tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult situation", and every one of them assumes a working life you have not had yet. If you are a student, a recent graduate, or changing careers into a field you have never worked in, this question format can feel like a locked door.

It is not. The assumption behind the panic is that competency examples have to come from paid work in the relevant field, and that assumption is simply wrong. Interviewers know they are talking to someone early in their career. They are not expecting a decade of anecdotes. They are testing whether you have the underlying behaviour, and that behaviour shows up everywhere, if you know how to look.

## The reframe that unlocks it

A competency question is not asking "when did you do this at work". It is asking "when have you ever done this". A time you led a team does not require a management title. A time you handled conflict does not require an office. The competency is a human behaviour, and you have been behaving like a human for twenty-odd years in dozens of settings.

The examples are hiding in five places most people never think to search:

- **Education.** Group projects, dissertations, presentations, being the person who organised the revision group, a module where something went wrong and you sorted it.
- **Part-time and casual work.** Any job at all, even one unrelated to your target career. A shift in a shop demonstrates the same competencies as a graduate scheme: dealing with people, working under pressure, handling a difficult customer, covering for a colleague.
- **Volunteering and societies.** Committee roles, running events, fundraising, coaching, mentoring. These are gold for leadership, teamwork, and initiative, precisely because you had no authority and did it anyway.
- **Sport and hobbies.** A team you played in, a target you trained towards, a club you helped run. Resilience, teamwork, and goal-setting all live here.
- **Personal and family life.** Organising something significant, supporting someone through a hard time, managing a genuine problem. Used carefully, these are completely legitimate.

Once you accept that all of these count, the door is open. Most people, once they actually list their groups, jobs, and activities, find they have too many examples, not too few.

## A worked example, from no work experience

Take the question "tell me about a time you showed leadership", answered by someone who has never had a job:

> "I would use my role organising our year's charity fundraiser at university. It was not an official position, I just ended up taking it on because it was drifting and nobody was steering it.
>
> We were three weeks out with barely anything booked and a committee that had lost momentum. I set a clear, simple goal, a single amount we were aiming to raise, because vague 'let's do well' targets had been part of the problem. Then I broke it into who was doing what, and I had a slightly awkward conversation with two people who had signed up and gone quiet, giving them a specific, small task rather than a vague ask.
>
> We hit the target, actually beat it by a bit, and more importantly the event ran smoothly instead of scrambling on the day. I learned that leading is mostly about turning a vague aim into specific asks that people can actually act on, and being willing to have the slightly uncomfortable follow-up conversations."

That is a genuine leadership answer, and there is not a single day of paid work in it. It has a decision, it moved other people, and it carried a real outcome. An interviewer scoring it does not care that it happened at a university fundraiser rather than in an office. The behaviour is the same.

## How to make non-work examples land

Three things make the difference between a non-work example that works and one that sounds like an excuse:

**Treat it with the same seriousness as a work example.** Do not apologise for it or frame it as "I know this is just a university thing, but...". The moment you diminish it, so will the interviewer. Tell it as a real example of the competency, because it is one.

**Use the [STAR structure](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) properly.** The structure is what makes a casual anecdote sound like professional evidence. Situation, task, action, result, the same as any other competency answer. It signals that you understand what the interviewer is looking for.

**Pull out the transferable point.** End by naming what the experience taught you in a way that connects to the working world. "I learned that turning a vague aim into specific asks is what actually moves a group" applies to a job as much as a fundraiser, and saying so shows you can see the bridge yourself.

## What to avoid

**Over-relying on personal or family examples.** They are legitimate, but one is plenty, and some are too personal for the room. Lean on education, work, and volunteering first, and keep personal examples for when you genuinely have no better option for a given competency.

**Apologising for your inexperience.** "I have not really done much yet" is a self-inflicted wound. The interviewer already knows your career stage. Your job is to show the behaviour, confidently, from wherever you have it.

**Inventing experience you do not have.** You do not need to. The whole point of this article is that you already have the material. Fabricated examples fall apart under follow-up questions, and the real ones are more than good enough.

## Build your bank before the interview

Spend an hour listing every group you have been part of, every job however small, every project, every activity. Then map your likely competency questions, leadership, teamwork, conflict, resilience, problem-solving, against that list. You will almost always find a real match. The [competency story bank guide](/blog/competency-interview-story-bank) walks through building this systematically.

Then rehearse the ones that felt weakest out loud, because non-work examples are the ones people deliver least confidently, and confidence is half of whether they land. You can [practise competency questions and get feedback](/practice) on whether your university, volunteering, and part-time examples come across as the genuine evidence they are.
