The Statement of Suitability is the part of a Civil Service application that candidates find hardest to get right, partly because the name is unhelpful. It sounds like a covering letter, so people write a covering letter: warm, general, and about how much they would like the job. Sifters do not score enthusiasm. They score evidence against the criteria in the advert.

Treated properly, the statement is closer to a structured evidence document than a letter, and the ones that succeed tend to look slightly mechanical compared with what people expect to write.

## What it is actually for

Adverts vary. Some campaigns ask for Behaviour statements only, some for a Statement of Suitability only, and many for both. Where a statement is requested, the advert will usually state a word limit, commonly between 500 and 1,250 words, and will tell you what to evidence against, typically the essential criteria in the person specification or a named set of Behaviours and Experience.

The sifter reads your statement with the criteria in front of them and marks against those criteria. That single fact should drive everything about how you write it. If a criterion is listed and your statement does not visibly address it, it scores zero, no matter how strong the rest is.

## Structure it against the advert, literally

The most reliable structure is the least creative one:

1. **A short opening**, two or three sentences, saying what you do now and why you are a credible fit. No throat clearing about your lifelong passion.
2. **One paragraph per essential criterion**, in the same order the advert lists them.
3. **A brief close**, two sentences, on motivation for this role and department.

Using subheadings that mirror the advert's wording is allowed in most campaigns and makes the sifter's job easy. A sifter working through a large volume of applications who can see immediately where each criterion is addressed is more likely to find and credit your evidence. Where the advert explicitly forbids subheadings, use the criterion wording as the opening phrase of each paragraph instead.

This feels blunt when you write it. It scores well.

## How much space to give each part

A rough allocation that works for a 1,000 word statement covering four criteria:

- Opening: 60 to 80 words
- Each criterion: roughly 200 words
- Close: 50 words

Within each criterion paragraph, the balance matters as much as the length. Aim for about a fifth on context, three fifths on what you specifically did, and a fifth on the result. Most weak statements invert this, spending half the paragraph explaining the organisational background before reaching any action.

## A worked extract

Take an essential criterion of "Experience of managing competing priorities in a fast paced environment", for an EO role.

> **Managing competing priorities**
>
> "In my current role I manage a caseload of around sixty active files alongside a weekly reporting duty and a rota commitment on the enquiry line, which frequently conflict.
>
> When our team lost a member in January, my caseload rose by roughly a third with no change to the reporting deadline. Rather than absorb it by working longer, I categorised the additional cases by statutory deadline and by whether the applicant was waiting on us or on a third party. That showed that about a quarter were genuinely dormant, waiting on external evidence, and could be moved to a fortnightly review rather than weekly.
>
> I proposed this to my line manager with the figures, and agreed a temporary change to the review cycle for that category only, which we reverted once the vacancy was filled. I flagged the change to the enquiry line team so they would not be surprised by a longer response time on those files.
>
> The outcome was that no statutory deadline was missed during the eleven weeks we were short staffed, and our average case age fell slightly, from thirty-one to twenty-nine days, because the freed capacity went to active files. My line manager subsequently used the same categorisation approach when covering a later absence."

**Why it works:** it opens with the scale of the competing demands in one sentence, then moves quickly to a specific decision with a stated basis. It shows the candidate proposing rather than just coping, communicating the consequence to an affected team, and reverting the change when the pressure passed, which reads as proportionate. Two numbers, one of which is a quality measure rather than a volume measure. And the final sentence shows the approach outlasting the incident, which is the sort of detail that lifts a score.

Note what is absent: no adjectives about being highly organised, no claim to thrive under pressure. The evidence does that work.

## The common failures

**Writing a covering letter.** Warmth, enthusiasm and a narrative about why the department appeals to you feel appropriate and score almost nothing. Motivation belongs in two sentences at the end.

**Not addressing a criterion.** Read the advert, list the essential criteria, and tick each one off against your draft. Missing one is the most expensive mistake available and it is entirely avoidable.

**Burying the evidence in prose.** Beautifully flowing paragraphs that weave several criteria together make the sifter hunt. Separate them.

**Unevidenced claims.** "I have excellent stakeholder management skills" is unmarkable. Replace every such sentence with what you did and what happened.

**Repeating your Behaviour statements.** If the campaign asks for both, use different examples. Reusing the same story wastes an opportunity to show breadth and reads as a thin evidence base.

**Going over, or well under, the word limit.** Over the limit risks being cut off or penalised. Well under is a wasted opportunity, and a 600 word statement against a 1,250 word limit usually means several criteria are thinly covered.

**Unexplained acronyms.** Sifters may not be from your department. Spell things out once.

## A checklist before you submit

- Every essential criterion in the advert has a visible home in the statement
- Each paragraph contains a specific action taken by you, in the first person
- At least half the criteria have a quantified or concrete outcome
- No sentence claims a quality without evidence attached
- Within the word limit, and using most of it
- No unexplained acronyms or internal team names
- Examples differ from those used in any Behaviour statements
- Read aloud once, to catch the sentences that collapse under their own subordinate clauses

## After the statement

If your statement gets you through the sift, expect the panel to interview against the same criteria, often probing the examples you used. That is a good reason to keep the written evidence accurate and to know each example well enough to answer follow up questions on it.

The interview stage adds Behaviours and usually Strengths.
