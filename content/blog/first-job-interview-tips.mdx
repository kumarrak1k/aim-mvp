Everyone has a first interview, and almost everyone walks into it with two disadvantages: no experience of interviews, and the belief that no experience of work means nothing to say. The first disadvantage is fixable in an afternoon. The second is simply untrue, and seeing why is most of the preparation.

This guide is for school leavers, students and anyone facing their first proper interview, for a part-time job, an [apprenticeship](/blog/apprenticeship-interview-tips), or a first full-time role.

## What a first interview is actually testing

Employers interviewing candidates without work history are not expecting work stories. They are checking four things:

- **Will you show up?** Reliability is the whole game for early roles. Anything that evidences it (attendance, a sports commitment kept for years, exams prepared for steadily) is directly relevant.
- **Will customers and colleagues like dealing with you?** Basic warmth, eye contact, listening properly.
- **Do you actually want this job**, or any job? A candidate who can say something specific about the company beats one who cannot, almost regardless of everything else.
- **Can you learn?** Not what you know: how you respond to being shown something new.

Notice that none of these need employment history. They need evidence from your actual life, told clearly.

## You have more material than you think

Interviewers accept examples from school, college, teams, volunteering, family responsibilities and hobbies. What matters is the shape: a situation, what you did, how it turned out. Some translations:

- Group coursework where someone was not pulling their weight: that is a teamwork and conflict example.
- Working a deadline for coursework while revising for exams: prioritisation under pressure.
- Helping at a family business, babysitting, coaching juniors at your club: responsibility and trust.
- Learning anything difficult on your own (an instrument, a language, code): self-driven learning.

Prepare four or five of these using the [STAR structure](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method), which is just a tidy way of saying beginning, middle, result. Keep each under two minutes.

## The questions you will almost certainly get

- **Tell me about yourself.** Thirty to sixty seconds: what you are doing now, one or two things you are proud of, why you are here. Full guide [here](/blog/how-to-answer-tell-me-about-yourself).
- **Why do you want to work here?** Have two specific reasons. For a shop or restaurant, having visited and noticed something real counts.
- **A strength and a weakness.** Pick a [genuine strength](/blog/how-to-answer-what-are-your-greatest-strengths) with an example, and a [real, minor weakness](/blog/how-to-answer-what-is-your-greatest-weakness) plus what you do about it.
- **Availability and practicalities.** Know your hours, term dates and travel plan before you sit down.

And prepare [two questions to ask them](/blog/questions-to-ask-at-the-end-of-an-interview): what does a good first month look like, what do you enjoy about working here. Asking nothing reads as wanting nothing.

## The practical layer

Dress one notch smarter than the job's daily wear ([guide](/blog/what-to-wear-to-an-interview)). Arrive ten minutes early, having done the journey maths honestly. Bring a copy of your CV, your phone on silent and in your pocket. Greet whoever meets you properly: reception impressions travel.

## Nerves, handled honestly

First-interview nerves are universal and interviewers know it; composure is graded on a curve for early roles. Two things genuinely help. First, rehearse out loud, at least twice, ideally to a person or a camera, because the first time you hear yourself answer should not be in the room. Our [practice interviews](/practice) exist for exactly this: realistic questions, spoken answers, feedback on what to tighten, in private, before it counts. Second, in the room, slow down. Nerves accelerate speech; a deliberate breath before each answer buys thinking time and reads as calm.

If a question stumps you, say that is a good question, can I think for a second, and take the second. If the interview goes badly, [it teaches you more than a good one](/blog/how-to-recover-from-a-bad-interview), and the second interview of your life is dramatically easier than the first. Everyone in the building once sat in your chair. Go show them the person who turns up.
