Adaptability answers have a hidden failure mode: they make the candidate sound passive. "The plan changed, so I went along with the new plan and it was fine." That is not adaptability, that is compliance. It tells the interviewer you can be moved around without complaint, which is not nothing, but it is not the skill they are hoping to find.

Genuine adaptability is active. When the ground shifts, you do something: you reassess, you find a new route, you let go of a plan you were invested in and rebuild quickly. The difference between "I coped with the change" and "I adapted to it" is whether you were steering or being carried. Interviewers can hear which one your story is.

## Why employers care about this now

Change-handling has become one of the most probed competencies, because most jobs now involve more of it than they used to: restructures, shifting priorities, new tools, projects that pivot halfway through. What a hiring manager fears is the person who is excellent as long as nothing changes and falls apart the moment it does. This question is them trying to find out whether you are that person.

So the answer needs to show not just that you accepted a change, but that you stayed effective through it, and ideally that you spotted the opportunity in it while others were still mourning the old plan.

## A worked example

> "I had a summer placement where I was assigned to build out a specific analysis for a client, and I spent the first week and a half deep into it. Then the client changed direction entirely, they had lost a big contract and their whole question changed, and most of what I had done was suddenly irrelevant.
>
> I will be honest, the first feeling was frustration, because I had put real work in. But sitting in that was not going to help anyone, so I made myself look at what had actually survived the change. And a chunk of it had. The data-cleaning and the structure I had built were still useful, even if the analysis on top of them was not.
>
> So instead of starting from zero, I flagged to my supervisor what could be reused and what genuinely had to be rebuilt, which meant we could give the client a realistic new timeline rather than a panicked one. I reframed the work around their new question over the next few days, reusing the foundation, and we delivered something they actually needed.
>
> The thing I learned was that when a plan collapses, the useful reflex is not 'start again', it is 'what still holds', because it is almost never all wasted, and finding the salvage first saves a huge amount of time."

## Why it works

The candidate is honest that the change was annoying, which is realistic and makes the rest credible. Nobody enjoys watching their work become irrelevant. But they do not stay there. The active move is deliberately assessing what survived rather than dramatically restarting, and that is a genuinely sophisticated response, the kind that distinguishes someone who adapts from someone who just accepts.

They also communicate, flagging to the supervisor what could be reused, which turns a personal adjustment into something that helped the whole delivery. And the closing insight, "what still holds", is a transferable principle rather than a platitude. It is the sort of line that makes an interviewer think this person will be calm and useful when their own team's plans change, which is exactly the reassurance they were seeking.

## The answers that fall flat

**Passive acceptance.** "They changed it, I adjusted, it was fine." No decision, no steering, nothing to score. Show what you actively did.

**Resisting the change as the story.** If your answer is really about how you fought the change or how wrong it was, you have demonstrated the opposite of adaptability. You can acknowledge a change was poorly handled, but the answer must be about how you got effective within the new reality, not how you dug in against it.

**A trivial change.** "The meeting moved to a different room" does not test anything. The change needs to have disrupted something real: your plan, your role, your workload, the goal itself.

**No effectiveness on the other side.** Adapting is not just surviving the transition. The interviewer wants to see you were still delivering after the change, not just that you emotionally recovered from it.

## Building your example

Look for a moment when something you were relying on changed underneath you: a project that pivoted, a role that shifted, a plan that fell through, a new system dropped on you, a client or tutor who changed the brief. The strongest versions have you doing something proactive in response, not just accepting. The [STAR framework](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) keeps it from wandering.

The single thing to check before the interview: is there an active verb in the middle of your story. Did you reassess, salvage, reframe, rebuild, flag. If the middle of your answer is passive ("so then the new plan happened"), the adaptability is missing. Say it aloud and listen for the point where you take action. You can [rehearse it and get told](/practice) whether your answer sounds like steering or drifting.
