Local government is one of the largest employers in the country, hiring across social care, planning, housing, finance, environmental services, communications and more. Its interviews sit somewhere between the [Civil Service](/blog/civil-service-interview-tips) and the private sector: structured and criteria-scored like Whitehall, but anchored to place, community and service delivery in a way national roles are not.

Candidates who walk in with generic public sector preparation miss that anchor, and it costs them, because the anchor is where councils score hardest.

## How council interviews are structured

Expect a panel (usually two to four people, often including the line manager and sometimes a councillor or service user for senior or public-facing roles), scoring every candidate against the same questions with written criteria. Answers are marked, not remembered: evidence per question matters more than overall impression.

The person specification in the job pack is the marking scheme, published in advance. Nearly every question maps to an essential criterion. Preparing a council interview without the person specification open beside you is preparing for a different interview.

## What councils score that others do not

- **Commitment to place.** Why this council, this area, these residents. Knowing something real about the borough (its demographics, a regeneration project, its budget pressures, something from the local news) separates candidates instantly. The council's corporate plan is public, short, and almost nobody reads it before interviewing.
- **Working with elected members.** For anything senior, expect a question about supporting councillors or handling political direction you personally disagree with. The credited answer respects the democratic mandate: officers advise honestly, members decide, officers deliver professionally, with a clear line only at legality and propriety.
- **Scarce money, real people.** Councils have spent over a decade doing more with less. Examples where you improved a service without new resources, prioritised transparently, or handled the human consequences of a constrained decision are worth gold.
- **Equality and safeguarding, meant seriously.** For public-facing and social care roles these are scored questions with wrong answers, not tick boxes. Know the safeguarding basics for your field, and have a genuine example of adapting a service or communication for different needs.

## Preparing your evidence

Build a [story bank](/blog/competency-interview-story-bank) mapped to the essential criteria, told in [STAR shape](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method), each with a result a panel can write down. Private sector experience translates well if you translate it: customers become residents and service users, revenue becomes value for money, stakeholders become members, partners and community groups.

Questions tend to be announced by criterion (this question is about partnership working), which is a gift: it tells you which story to reach for. Panels are also usually comfortable repeating a question or giving you a moment; ask, rather than answering the question you half-heard.

## The practical details

Some councils send questions in advance (increasingly common as an inclusion practice); if they do, prepare all of them and rehearse aloud rather than drafting essays. Presentations on a service problem are common at officer grades and above: the [presentation guide](/blog/presentation-interview-tips) applies, with one addition: always cover the resident impact, not just the organisational fix.

Scoring is often shared on request afterwards, and council recruitment genuinely does reinterview internal and external candidates on level terms, so [asking for feedback](/blog/how-to-get-interview-feedback) after a miss is worth more here than almost anywhere: you will usually get criterion-level detail you can fix.

Rehearse the spoken versions of your evidence before the day: [practice interviews](/practice) with feedback on structure and delivery turn a criteria list and a story bank into answers a panel can score highly, which is the whole game in structured interviewing.
