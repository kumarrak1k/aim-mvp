Most assessment centre advice explains what the exercises are. This guide assumes you know ([start here](/blog/what-to-expect-at-an-assessment-centre) if not) and answers the harder question: with two weeks and limited hours, what does useful practice actually look like?

The principle throughout: assessment centres score behaviour, and behaviour only changes through repetition under something like real conditions. Reading is orientation. Practice is doing the exercise, timed, and reviewing against criteria.

## First: find out your schedule

The invitation email usually lists the exercises. Everything below assumes the common graduate set: case study, competency interview, presentation, group exercise, sometimes a written exercise or role play. Weight your hours by two things: which exercises your schedule includes, and which you have never done live. An exercise you have never once done under a clock deserves triple the time of one you have.

## The case study: three timed runs

The skill is a repeatable skeleton (recommendation, reasons anchored to exhibits, the counter-argument, risks and next steps) executed fast. It becomes automatic at around the third timed attempt, which is why one practice run is nearly useless and three are transformative.

Run one: expect to blow the timing; the point is calibration. Run two: hold the skeleton. Run three: full exam conditions. Review each against assessor criteria ([listed here](/blog/assessment-centre-case-study-tips)), scoring yourself harshly on did I decide and did I use the numbers.

Our [mock assessment centre](/assessment-centre) runs the written case study against the clock with scoring on exactly those criteria, which removes the self-marking problem.

## The competency interview: stories out loud, not notes on paper

You need six to eight stories covering the usual ground (teamwork, pressure, conflict, initiative, failure), each tellable in about two minutes. Build the [story bank](/blog/competency-interview-story-bank) once, then spend your remaining time speaking, because the gap between a story on paper and the same story aloud is where interviews are lost.

Practise answering questions you have not pre-matched to stories: the live skill is selecting and adapting, not reciting. [Scored practice interviews](/practice) are built for precisely this loop: unseen question, spoken answer, feedback on structure and delivery, again.

## The presentation: rehearse the mechanics, not just the content

Whether you get the brief in advance or on the day, the marks concentrate in the same places: a clear message stated early, a structure the audience can hear, timing that lands inside the limit, and composure in the questions afterwards.

Rehearse aloud, standing, timed, at least twice; on camera if the centre is virtual. The questions after the presentation are usually worth as many marks as the presentation: practise being pushed on your recommendation without folding or bristling. Full guide [here](/blog/presentation-interview-tips).

## The group exercise: practise behaviours, not dominance

You cannot rehearse a group without a group, but you can decide and drill your behaviours: bring in quiet members by name, summarise every ten minutes, propose structure early (shall we take two minutes each, then prioritise), watch the clock aloud. Pick two of those and make them habitual; that is enough to be visible. The [full guide](/blog/group-exercise-interview-tips) covers the scoring.

If your schedule includes [role play](/blog/role-play-exercise-assessment-centre) or a [written exercise](/blog/written-exercise-assessment-centre), the same logic applies: one read of the guide, then live reps with a friend playing awkward or a 20-minute timed brief.

## The final 48 hours

Stop adding new material two days out; late cramming buys anxiety, not marks. Instead:

- One light run of your presentation and your two strongest stories, aloud.
- Logistics done properly: route or [tech setup](/blog/virtual-assessment-centre-tips) tested, clothes decided, brief reread.
- Sleep, genuinely prioritised. Assessment days are long, and the afternoon exercises are where tired candidates leak marks.

On the day itself, treat every minute as scored (corridors and lunch included, informally), reset between exercises rather than conducting post-mortems, and remember that assessors are comparing you against nerves-adjusted reality, not perfection.

Two weeks of this (perhaps eight focused hours) puts you ahead of the large majority of candidates, who will have read everything and rehearsed nothing. The reading is the map. The practice is the journey. Do the reps.
