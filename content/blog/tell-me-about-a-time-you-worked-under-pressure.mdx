A lot of candidates answer this question by describing how stressful the situation was, in detail, as if the stress itself were the achievement. The deadline was impossible, everything was going wrong, they were up until 3am. And then, somehow, it got done. The interviewer is left with a vivid picture of a crisis and only the vaguest idea of what the candidate actually did to get through it.

That is the mistake. The pressure is the setup, not the story. What the interviewer is scoring is the thing you did when the heat was on: did you prioritise, did you communicate, did you make a decision, or did you just work faster and hope. The best answers spend one sentence on the pressure and the rest on the method.

## What good looks like under pressure

There is a difference between someone who survives pressure and someone who manages it, and interviewers can hear it. Survival sounds like effort. Management sounds like judgement. The judgement usually takes one of these forms:

- **You reprioritised.** You worked out what actually mattered and let the less important things slide, deliberately, rather than trying to do everything and failing at all of it.
- **You communicated early.** You flagged the risk to the people who needed to know before it became a surprise, and managed expectations instead of going quiet and hoping.
- **You made a clean decision under uncertainty.** You picked a path when there was no time to be sure, and owned it.

A strong answer shows at least one of these clearly. "I worked really hard and pulled it off" shows none of them, which is why it lands flat despite being true.

## A worked example

> "In my final year, two big deadlines landed in the same week, a dissertation chapter and a group presentation for a different module, and then one of my group members dropped out three days before the presentation, taking their whole section with them.
>
> The instinct was to panic and try to do everything, but I forced myself to stop and think about what actually could not slip. The dissertation chapter had a hard deadline and only I could do it. The presentation was shared, and the missing section was a problem for the group, not just me.
>
> So I did two things. I protected the dissertation time by blocking out two mornings and refusing to touch anything else in them. And I messaged the group straight away rather than waiting, and we agreed to absorb the missing section by trimming it down and splitting the key points between the three of us, rather than one person trying to rebuild it whole.
>
> Both got done. The chapter was not my best work but it was solid and on time, and the presentation went fine because we had been honest early instead of scrambling on the day. What I learned was that under pressure the temptation is to speed up, but the thing that actually saves you is stopping for ten minutes to decide what to drop."

## Why it works

The candidate spends two sentences on the pressure and then pivots hard into method. They prioritised (the dissertation could not slip, only they could do it), they communicated early (messaged the group straight away), and they made a proportionate call (trim and split, rather than rebuild). Every one of those is a decision, and decisions are what get scored.

The honesty about the chapter, "not my best work but solid and on time", is quietly powerful. It shows the candidate understands that under real pressure, good-enough-and-delivered beats perfect-and-late, which is a genuinely mature judgement and one a lot of high achievers struggle with. And the closing line names the actual lesson: slowing down to prioritise beats speeding up. That is the insight the whole answer was building to.

## Common misfires

**All crisis, no method.** If your answer is a dramatic account of how hard it was, with the resolution compressed into "and then I got it done", you have described a problem, not shown a skill.

**Thriving on chaos.** Saying "I love working under pressure, I do my best work at the last minute" is a trap. It makes you sound like someone who creates the pressure they claim to handle, and no manager wants that. Composure is attractive. Adrenaline-seeking is not.

**Superhuman effort as the answer.** "I just worked through the night" is not a strategy, it is the absence of one. Occasionally it is necessary, but if it is the whole story, the interviewer learns you had no better option than brute force.

**No prioritisation.** The single most valuable thing to demonstrate here is choosing what not to do. If your answer has you doing everything and succeeding, it either is not true or the pressure was not real.

## Building your example

The best pressure stories have a genuine collision: two things at once, a deadline that moved, a resource that vanished. Look for a moment where you could not do everything and had to choose. Jobs, projects, exams, and volunteering all produce these regularly. The [STAR structure](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) keeps it tight so you do not disappear into describing the chaos.

The discipline this answer needs is compression: one sentence of pressure, then straight to what you did. Most people overshoot the setup by a mile. Say it aloud and time yourself, and if the crisis is taking longer to describe than the response, cut it back. You can [rehearse the balance](/practice) and get told when the setup is running long.
