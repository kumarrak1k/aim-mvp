Candidates prepare hard for Civil Service Behaviours and then get caught out by Strengths, because they treat them as the same thing with a different label. They are not. Behaviours ask for evidence of what you have done. Strengths ask what you are naturally good at and energised by, and they are deliberately designed so that you cannot rehearse a specific answer.

If you walk into a Strengths section with four polished STAR examples, you will find they do not fit the questions, and forcing them in is precisely what makes an answer score badly.

## What Strengths are actually assessing

The Civil Service works from a published set of strengths, each describing something a person does well and enjoys doing. Examples include Analyst, Improver, Organiser, Explainer, Team Leader, Problem Solver, Relationship Builder, Challenger and Precise, among a longer list.

The advert will usually not tell you which Strengths are being assessed. That is intentional. The theory is that genuine strengths show up consistently and spontaneously, so an unrehearsed answer is a better signal than a prepared one. Panels are listening for three things:

- **Authenticity.** Does this sound like something you actually do, or something you decided to claim.
- **Energy.** Strengths are as much about what motivates you as what you are competent at. Competence without enthusiasm reads as a learned behaviour rather than a strength.
- **Consistency.** Does the picture hold together across several answers, or does the candidate claim to be both a meticulous completer-finisher and someone energised by constant change.

That last point matters more than people expect. Strengths questions come in a run, and panels notice when the self-portrait is internally contradictory.

## What the questions look like

Strengths questions are short, present tense, and often about preference rather than achievement:

- What do you enjoy most about your current role?
- Do you prefer starting things or finishing them?
- What sort of task makes time pass quickly for you?
- Are you at your best working alone or with others?
- What would your colleagues come to you for help with?
- What do you find draining?
- How do you feel when a plan changes at short notice?

Notice that several have no obviously "correct" answer. "Do you prefer starting or finishing" is not a trick: the Civil Service needs both, and the panel is calibrating who you are rather than checking a box. Trying to guess the desired answer is the main way candidates go wrong, because guessed answers tend to be bland and inconsistent with the rest of the interview.

Note also the negatively framed ones. "What do you find draining" is a real question, and answering it with a non-answer such as "nothing really, I enjoy all of it" is worse than answering honestly. It reads as evasive and it fails the authenticity test.

## How to answer well

The structure is much lighter than STAR. A good Strengths answer is usually three short beats, delivered in under a minute:

1. **A direct answer.** Say the thing plainly.
2. **A brief, concrete illustration.** One or two sentences, not a full STAR story.
3. **The why.** What it is about that work that you find satisfying.

That third beat is the one candidates omit, and it is where the strength actually shows.

> **"What do you enjoy most about your current role?"**
>
> "Honestly, the part I enjoy most is taking something messy and making it legible for other people. In my current job that usually means our monthly performance pack, which used to be about thirty pages of tables that almost nobody read properly.
>
> I rebuilt it so the first page answers the three questions people actually ask, and the detail sits behind it for anyone who wants it. I get a slightly disproportionate amount of satisfaction from that, and I know it is a genuine preference rather than just a task I was given, because I do the same thing unprompted with meeting notes and with my own to-do list.
>
> What I like about it is that it is quietly useful. Nobody thanks you for a clear document, but a lot of poor decisions come from people not understanding the information in front of them."

**Why this works:** it answers directly, illustrates briefly rather than launching into a full example, and then explains the motivation. The line about doing the same thing unprompted elsewhere is the strongest part, because consistency across contexts is exactly the evidence a Strengths assessor is listening for. And the closing thought is a real opinion rather than a platitude.

## The mistakes that lose marks

**Delivering a prepared Behaviour example.** The most common error. A two minute STAR answer to "what do you enjoy" signals that you are working from a script, and it usually does not answer the question asked.

**Claiming every strength.** Answering as though you are energised by everything makes the whole set unreliable. Panels are building a profile, and a profile with no shape is not credible.

**Refusing to admit anything you dislike.** If asked what drains you, name something real and modest, and ideally show how you manage it. "I find long unstructured meetings hard going, so I have got into the habit of asking for an agenda in advance or offering to write one" is honest, self-aware, and shows you handle it.

**Contradicting yourself across answers.** Saying you thrive on autonomy and later that you get your energy from constant collaboration will be noticed. Think in advance about the two or three things that are genuinely true of you, and let your answers be consistent with them.

**Over-long answers.** Strengths sections move quickly and may cover many questions. Sixty to ninety seconds is usually right. Running long here also eats the time available for the rest of the interview.

## How to prepare when you cannot prepare answers

You cannot rehearse Strengths answers, but you can absolutely prepare, and candidates who do are noticeably more fluent.

**Do the self-audit.** Over a week, notice which tasks you start early and which you put off. That is better evidence about your genuine strengths than introspection, because it is behavioural. Write down four or five things you consistently gravitate towards.

**Find the honest weak side.** Identify two things you find genuinely draining and how you cope. Having these ready means the negatively framed questions do not knock you off balance.

**Collect small illustrations, not big stories.** For each strength, have a one sentence concrete example ready. These are the connective tissue that stops answers becoming abstract.

**Check your consistency.** Look at your list and ask whether it describes a coherent person. If it contains obvious tensions, decide which is more true of you.

**Practise the rhythm, not the content.** The skill is answering an unexpected question crisply in under a minute with a real illustration. That is a rhythm you can drill even without knowing the questions, and it is the single most transferable preparation for a Strengths section. ## How Strengths sit alongside the rest

Strengths are scored on the same scale as Behaviours, so the same [scoring logic](/blog/how-civil-service-applications-are-scored) applies, but the evidence base is entirely different. Behaviours are past and evidenced; Strengths are present tense and about disposition. Most Civil Service interviews mix both, often with Behaviours first, and the shift can be jarring if you are not expecting it.

Prepare your [Behaviour examples](/blog/civil-service-behaviours-examples) thoroughly, because those can and should be rehearsed. Then prepare for Strengths differently: know yourself, keep answers short, and resist the urge to perform.

The most useful rehearsal for Strengths is being asked something you have not anticipated and having to answer it in a minute without waffling. You can [practise responding to unexpected questions and get feedback](/practice) on length and filler, which is where unprepared answers usually come apart.
