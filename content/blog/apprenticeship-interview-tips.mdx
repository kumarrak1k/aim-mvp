The most common mistake in an apprenticeship interview is trying to sound like someone with a career behind them. Candidates pad thin experience, borrow corporate phrases they have read online, and end up sounding rehearsed rather than keen. It rarely works, because the employer already knows exactly how much experience you have. They can see your age and your application. Pretending otherwise just makes them wonder what else you are overstating.

Apprenticeship recruiters are doing something quite specific: hiring for potential over a two to four year commitment, where they will fund your training and carry you while you are not yet productive. That changes what they look for, and once you understand it the preparation becomes much simpler.

## What they are actually assessing

Three things, roughly in this order, and none of them is technical knowledge.

**Will you stick with it.** Apprenticeships have a real dropout problem, and a candidate who leaves after five months costs the employer training levy money, a wasted place and a supervisor's time. So the question underneath many of their questions is: does this person know what they are signing up for, and have they chosen it deliberately rather than defaulting to it. Evidence that you have researched the route, understood the day-to-day work, and can explain why it suits you beats enthusiasm every time.

**Are you coachable.** You will be taught almost everything. An apprentice who takes correction badly is a much bigger problem than one who starts slow, because the whole model depends on being trainable. Any example where you were told you had something wrong, took it on board and changed is worth more here than an example of you already being brilliant.

**Will you turn up and be reliable.** Unglamorous, and genuinely near the top of the list. Attendance, punctuality, doing what you said you would do. If you have a Saturday job, a sports commitment you have kept for years, or a volunteering role with a rota, that is direct evidence, and most candidates never think to mention it.

## Experience you do not think counts

You almost certainly have more usable material than you believe. Employers know you have not run a department. What they want is evidence of the behaviour, from wherever you have it:

- **School and college.** A group project where the workload was uneven, a deadline you had to manage around exams, a subject you found hard and worked at anyway.
- **Part-time and weekend work.** Retail, hospitality, cleaning, a paper round, helping in a family business. All of it demonstrates reliability, customer contact and working under pressure.
- **Sport, music, cadets, clubs.** Long commitment, coaching younger members, training through a setback, a team you captained.
- **Home and caring responsibilities.** Looking after siblings, helping a relative, translating for a family member. Used carefully, these are legitimate and often show real maturity.

The [competency structure](/blog/how-to-pass-a-competency-interview) works on all of these, and there is a fuller guide to [answering when you have no formal experience](/blog/how-to-answer-competency-questions-with-no-experience) if that is your worry.

## A worked example

Take a question you are very likely to be asked: "Why do you want to do an apprenticeship rather than go to university?"

> "I looked at both properly, and the honest answer is that I learn much better by doing something than by reading about it. I found that out in my BTEC, where the coursework units where we actually built something were the ones I did well in, and the purely written units were the ones I had to grind through.
>
> I also went to an open evening at a company doing something similar to this, and talked to a second-year apprentice there. What sold it was hearing how much responsibility she had by year two, and that she was working on real jobs rather than practice ones. She was honest about the hard parts too, that the first six months were a lot of being the person who does not know anything yet, and that the college work on top of a full week is tiring.
>
> Knowing that made me more sure rather than less, because it did not sound like something I would resent. I would rather be tired doing something I can see the point of. And the qualification at the end plus four years of actual experience seemed like a better position to be in at twenty-two than a degree on its own."

**Why it works:** it gives a real reason grounded in self-knowledge, not a slogan about preferring the "real world". The candidate has done concrete research, including talking to an actual apprentice, and repeats the downsides they were told, which proves the research happened and that they are going in with open eyes. That directly answers the recruiter's fear about dropout. The closing point about position at twenty-two is a considered judgement rather than a boast.

## Questions you should expect

- Why this apprenticeship, and why this company specifically
- What do you understand the job to involve day to day
- Tell us about a time you worked in a team
- Tell us about something you found difficult and how you handled it
- How do you organise your time when you have a lot on
- What are you hoping to be doing in five years
- How do you feel about doing college or university work alongside a full working week

That last one is asked more often than candidates expect, and answering it with "I'll just work hard" is weak. Say something concrete about how you already balance commitments now.

## The mistakes that cost places

**Not researching the actual company.** Applying to twelve schemes and knowing nothing specific about any of them is transparent, and it is the single biggest differentiator at this level because so few candidates do it. Ten minutes on what the company makes or does, who its customers are, and one recent piece of news puts you ahead of most of the room. The [company research guide](/blog/how-to-research-a-company-before-an-interview) covers how.

**Treating it as a fallback.** Any hint that this is what you are doing because your grades were not what you hoped, or because university did not work out, is damaging. Even if that is partly true, the answer needs to be about what draws you to this route, not what pushed you from another one.

**Saying you have no experience.** You have some. Saying otherwise does your own application harm, and it is usually not accurate, just unexamined.

**One-word answers.** Nerves make young candidates shrink their answers to a sentence. An interviewer with nothing to score cannot score you well. Aim for a proper example with a beginning, a middle and an outcome, using the [STAR structure](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) to keep it organised.

**Having no questions at the end.** Ask something real: what does a typical first year look like, what do apprentices find hardest, what happens after the apprenticeship finishes. It signals you are picturing yourself there.

## If there is an assessment day

Larger employers, particularly engineering, finance and the big training providers, often run a group assessment rather than a single interview. Expect a group task, sometimes a short presentation, sometimes a numeracy or literacy test. The group exercise is where places are won and lost, and the thing being watched is not whether you dominate but whether you make the group work better. Bringing in a quieter person, keeping an eye on the time, summarising where the group has got to: those are the behaviours that get noted. There is more detail in the [group exercise guide](/blog/group-exercise-interview-tips) and on [what to expect at an assessment centre](/blog/what-to-expect-at-an-assessment-centre).

## Preparing without overthinking it

You do not need a script. You need four or five real stories from school, work or your own life that you can tell clearly, plus a genuine reason for wanting this specific apprenticeship. Say them out loud before the day, because the gap between having a story in your head and telling it under pressure is wider than anyone expects, and it is the thing that makes nervous candidates trail off mid-sentence.

You can [practise apprenticeship-style questions and get feedback](/practice) on structure, length and filler before you walk in. For most people the first honest playback is a surprise, and fixing it takes one session rather than weeks.
