First, the reframe that matters: if you are getting interviews but not offers, your CV works and your experience is good enough. Employers are inviting you in. The problem is happening in the room, in the space between "impressive on paper" and "the person we chose", and that is genuinely good news, because what happens in the room is far more fixable than your CV or your background.

The frustrating part is that the reason is usually specific and invisible to you. You walk out thinking it went fine, and it did, mostly, but something quietly cost you, and nobody tells you what. Here are the seven reasons this actually happens, in rough order of how common they are, so you can work out which is yours.

## 1. Your answers are fine but forgettable

This is the most common reason, and the hardest to see, because nothing goes wrong. You answer every question competently. You do not fumble, you do not freeze, you are perfectly pleasant. And you are completely interchangeable with the four other competent, pleasant candidates, so the interviewer has no reason to fight for you.

Forgettable usually means generic: adjectives instead of evidence, "we" instead of "I", stories with no specific detail or number, answers that could have come from anyone. The fix is not to be more polished, it is to be more specific and more you. One vivid, concrete, slightly personal answer that the interviewer remembers at the debrief beats five smooth ones they cannot recall.

## 2. You are answering competency questions without structure

If your "tell me about a time" answers wander, the interviewer cannot follow them, and an answer they cannot follow cannot score well even if the underlying story is strong. Rambling, no clear situation, an action buried in the middle, no result: these are the mechanics that sink otherwise good candidates.

This is the single most fixable thing on the list. Learning to structure answers with the [STAR method](/blog/how-to-use-the-star-method) so the interviewer can actually follow and score them is often the difference between "nice person, no offer" and an offer. If you keep failing behavioural-heavy interviews specifically, this is very likely your issue.

## 3. You are running long and losing the room

Related but distinct. Even structured answers fail if they are twice as long as they should be, because the interviewer stops listening around the two-minute mark and your best point, delivered at ninety seconds, gets buried under thirty seconds of padding. People have almost no ability to judge their own answer length under pressure, so this one is nearly invisible from the inside. If interviewers seem to lose engagement partway through your answers, [length](/blog/how-long-should-interview-answers-be) is a likely culprit.

## 4. Your delivery is undercutting your words

Sometimes the content is good but the delivery leaks doubt. Filler words ("um", "like", "sort of") stacking up, a voice that trails off at the end of sentences so every point sounds like a question, a pace so fast it reads as nervous, or no eye contact on video. The interviewer may not consciously register any of it, but they come away with a vague sense that you were not quite convincing, and that sense costs you.

The gap here is that you cannot hear your own filler or feel your own pace in the moment. It has to be shown to you. This is one of the most common hidden reasons confident, capable people underperform, and it is genuinely invisible without feedback.

## 5. You are not answering the actual question

Under pressure, a lot of candidates hear the first few words of a question, latch onto a rehearsed answer, and deliver it, even when it does not quite fit what was asked. The interviewer notices. It reads as either not listening or not being able to think on your feet, both of which are quietly disqualifying. If you tend to pre-load answers, this may be happening without your knowledge. The fix is listening to the whole question and taking a beat before answering.

## 6. You have not given them a reason you want this job specifically

Employers are wary of candidates who seem to want a job, any job, rather than this one. If your "why this company" and "why this role" answers are generic and could apply to any employer, you register as a flight risk, someone who will leave the moment something else comes along. Interviewers would rather hire someone slightly less qualified who clearly wants to be there than someone brilliant who seems indifferent. Specific, researched reasons for wanting this particular role close this gap.

## 7. Nerves are flattening you

Sometimes the honest answer is that anxiety is compressing everything: your personality disappears, your answers get shorter and more robotic, and the warm, capable person who exists in normal life never shows up in the room. The interviewer meets a tense, guarded version of you and cannot see what your friends and colleagues see. This is real, common, and not a character flaw. It is a familiarity problem: interviews feel high-stakes and rare, so your body treats each one as a threat. The only reliable fix is [making them feel routine](/blog/interview-nerves-how-to-stay-calm) through repetition.

## The thread running through all of these

Look at the seven and notice something: almost none of them are about your qualifications or your experience. They are about how you come across in the room, structure, specificity, length, delivery, listening, motivation, composure. That is why "keep failing interviews" is such a hopeful problem to have. You do not need a different background. You need to fix a small number of specific, in-the-room habits, most of which you cannot currently see.

And that last part is the real obstacle. You cannot diagnose your own delivery, length, or filler from the inside, which is why reading this only gets you so far. You need to be shown. The fastest way to find your reason is to run realistic practice and get the specifics fed back: where you rambled, where the filler stacked up, where the answer lost structure, where you did not answer the question. You can [practise real interview questions and get that exact feedback](/practice), and for most people, one honest look at their own answers reveals the culprit within a session or two. Once you can see it, you can fix it, and the rejections usually stop being a mystery.
