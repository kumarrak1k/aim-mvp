A retail or hospitality interview is usually short, sometimes fifteen minutes, often conducted by a store or venue manager between other tasks, and occasionally on the shop floor. That format misleads a lot of candidates into thinking it is casual and needs no preparation. It is not casual. It is compressed, which means the margins are thinner: a manager forms a view very quickly, and there is less time to recover from a poor start than in a structured hour-long interview.

The good news is that what they are looking for is narrow and knowable, and very few candidates prepare for it at all. Doing thirty minutes of preparation puts you clearly ahead.

## What managers are actually hiring for

**Reliability above everything.** A retail or hospitality manager's worst recurring problem is shifts that go uncovered. Someone competent who calls in sick every third Saturday is worse than someone average who always turns up. Any evidence of dependability, a job you held for a year, a rota you never missed, a commitment you kept through something difficult, goes directly at their biggest fear.

**How you behave when a customer is unhappy.** Not whether you are polite when things are calm, which everyone is, but what you do when someone is rude, wrong, or upset in front of other customers.

**Whether you notice work.** The difference between a good and a mediocre floor employee is largely whether they see the thing that needs doing without being told: the queue building, the shelf gone bare, the table not cleared, the customer hovering. Managers describe this as "using your initiative", and they will ask about it.

**Whether you fit the pace and the team.** A quiet bookshop and a Friday night bar require different temperaments. Be honest about which suits you, because being miscast is miserable and short-lived.

## Availability: answer this carefully

Availability is often the real decision criterion, and candidates handle it badly by being vague in the hope of seeming flexible. Managers read vagueness as unreliability waiting to happen.

Be specific and honest. If you cannot work Sunday mornings, say so plainly and say what you can do. A candidate who says "I can do any evening Monday to Thursday, all day Saturday, and Sundays after two" is far easier to hire than one who says "I'm pretty flexible" and then turns down half the rota. Weekend and peak availability, particularly December in retail and weekend evenings in hospitality, is often what actually decides it.

## A worked example: the difficult customer

> "I worked in a garden centre café at weekends. A customer came back to the till fairly angry because she had waited about twenty minutes for a toastie and it had come out wrong, and she had a child with her who was getting upset.
>
> The first thing I did was not explain. I apologised properly and asked her to sit back down and said I would bring it over rather than making her queue again, mostly because she had a small child and standing at the counter was making it worse for both of them.
>
> Then I checked what had actually happened, which was that we had two tickets with the same name and the kitchen had made the wrong one. So I got hers made straight away, took it over myself, and took the cost off, which I could do up to a fiver without asking a manager.
>
> She was fine about it by then, and she came back the following weekend, which felt like the real result. Afterwards I mentioned the duplicate ticket thing to my supervisor, because it had happened once before, and we started putting a table number on the ticket instead of just a name.
>
> The bit I took from it was that most of her annoyance was about being ignored for twenty minutes rather than the sandwich. Once she could see someone had taken it seriously, the food was almost beside the point."

**Why it works:** it opens with a concrete action, moving her away from the counter, rather than a claim about staying calm. It shows the candidate knew the limit of their own authority, the five pound discretion, which managers like because it means they will neither freeze nor overstep. It fixes the underlying cause rather than just the incident. And the closing observation, that the anger was about being ignored, is real insight into customer service rather than a platitude. There is a fuller treatment of this question in the [difficult customer guide](/blog/describe-a-time-you-dealt-with-a-difficult-customer).

## Questions you should expect

- Why do you want to work here specifically
- What would you do if a customer was unhappy and you could not fix it
- How do you cope when it is very busy
- Tell me about a time you worked in a team
- What would you do if you saw a colleague taking something
- What is your availability, including weekends and Christmas
- Have you used a till or booking system before

The honesty question, about a colleague stealing, comes up more than people expect in retail and it has a right answer: you report it, through the proper channel, without confronting them yourself. Shrinkage is a serious cost and managers need to know where you stand.

## The "why here" question actually matters

Even for a part-time role, the manager wants to hear something specific. "I need a job and you had a vacancy" is honest and forgettable. Something as simple as being a regular customer, liking how the place is run, having heard the team is good, or preferring this chain's approach to a competitor's, is enough. Ten minutes looking at the company's own website, their values page and their current promotions is more preparation than most candidates do, and it shows immediately.

## Common mistakes

**Turning up dressed wrong.** Judge it by the venue. Smart casual is right for most retail and hospitality; a suit can look out of place in a coffee shop and scruffy trainers look careless in a department store. The [what to wear guide](/blog/what-to-wear-to-an-interview) covers the general rule, which is to dress slightly smarter than the staff you can see working there.

**Saying you have no experience.** If you have never had a paid job, you still have examples: school, sport, volunteering, helping at home. Reliability and dealing with people show up in all of them. See the [no experience guide](/blog/how-to-answer-competency-questions-with-no-experience).

**Being negative about a previous employer.** "The manager was awful" is an immediate red flag, because the interviewer assumes they will be described that way next. Keep it neutral and forward-looking.

**Undersellin­g weekend availability when you do have it.** If you can work the busy times, say so early. It is often the single most valuable thing about your application.

**No questions at the end.** Ask what a typical shift looks like, how many hours you would realistically get, who you would report to, and whether there is training. It signals you intend to stay.

## A note on trial shifts

Some hospitality employers ask for a paid trial shift. In the UK, if you are doing real work that benefits the business, you should normally be paid for it. A short unpaid observation is different from working a full service unpaid. It is reasonable to ask politely whether the trial is paid, and how long it will be, before agreeing. A good employer will answer straightforwardly.

## Preparing in half an hour

Pick three examples: one about a customer, one about being busy or under pressure, one about working with others. Know your availability precisely. Look at the company website for ten minutes. Then say your three examples out loud once, because in a fifteen-minute interview a rambling answer costs you proportionally far more than it would in an hour.

You can [practise these questions and hear how you come across](/practice) before the day, which is the fastest way to find out whether your answers are landing or trailing off.
